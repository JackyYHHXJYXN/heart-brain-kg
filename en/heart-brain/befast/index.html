Heart and brain · Recognising symptoms

Spotting a stroke: FAST

If the face, an arm or speech suddenly goes wrong, treat it as an emergency and call your local emergency number straight away. The US CDC's BE FAST adds balance and eyesight checks.

Grade A · when to get care

If any of these happen, get care promptly

  • If any of these appears, call your local emergency number for an ambulance. Do not drive yourself, and do not have someone drive you.[1][2][3][4]
  • If the signs pass after a short time, or have already gone, still call your local emergency number straight away.[1][5][2][4][3]

04

Explanation

  • Look at the face: if one side suddenly droops, or the person tries to smile and one side will not move, think stroke.[1][2][3]
  • Check the arms: ask the person to lift both. If one arm is weak or numb, cannot stay up or drifts down, think stroke.[1][2][3]
  • Listen to speech: if it suddenly becomes slurred, strange or confused, or the person cannot understand what others say, think stroke.[2][1][3][4][5]
  • Balance: sudden dizziness, feeling off-balance or falling over can also be a sign of stroke.[2][4][5][1]
  • Eyes: sudden blurred vision or loss of sight in one or both eyes can also be a sign of stroke.[2][1][4][5]
  • Note when the symptoms first started and tell the ambulance crew.[2][4]
  • A sudden severe headache can also be a sign of stroke.[2][5][4][1]
Expand / collapse · 1 paragraphs

In the UK the usual prompt is FAST (face, arms, speech, time). The US CDC uses BE FAST, which adds balance (B) and eyes (E). Both are prompts; any sudden change should be treated as an emergency.[1][3][2]
